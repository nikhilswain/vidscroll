vidscroll ties a video to the page scroll. While you scroll through it, the
video stays pinned to the screen and moves forward or backward with you, frame
by frame. Text and other content can appear over it at chosen moments.

Scroll inside the example to try it. The strip underneath shows where you are
in the video and which part of the story is on screen.

## What you write

A video file, how much scrolling it should take, and the content to show
along the way:

```tsx title="Hero.tsx"
import { ScrollVideo, Section } from "vidscroll";

export function Hero() {
  return (
    <ScrollVideo src="/videos/hero.mp4" length="400vh">
      <Section start={0} end={0.3}>
        <h2>Doors close</h2>
      </Section>
    </ScrollVideo>
  );
}
```

React 18 and 19 are supported, including Next.js. For other frameworks or plain
HTML there is an experimental `<vid-scroll>` element.

## Why scroll-driven video is hard

Playing a video forward is cheap. Jumping around in one is not, and scrolling
means jumping on every frame.

Videos are stored as occasional complete pictures, called **keyframes**, with
only the changes in between. To show any frame, the browser has to start from
the keyframe before it and work forward. Most exported videos have keyframes a
few seconds apart, because that makes files smaller. Each small scroll then
asks the browser to decode dozens of frames before it can show one, which looks
like stutter. Scrolling backwards is worse.

vidscroll deals with this in two ways:

- **Automatically.** It checks how far apart a video's keyframes are. If they
  are too far apart, it re-encodes the video in the browser with closely spaced
  keyframes, keeps the result in the browser's cache, and swaps it in at the
  same frame. You can scroll the original in the meantime.
- **Ahead of time.** `npx vidscroll encode hero.mp4` prepares the file before
  you deploy it, so visitors never wait for the re-encode.

[How it works](/docs/start/how-it-works/) explains both in detail.

## When to use it

vidscroll fits pages where the reader controls the pace of a video:

- product pages and launch pages that walk through an object or a feature
- storytelling and scrollytelling, with text tied to scenes
- landing page heroes that respond to the first scroll
- chapter-based scenes you can jump between

It is less suited to:

- **Videos with sound.** Scroll-driven video is always muted, and the
  in-browser re-encode drops the audio.
- **Long videos.** Anything over about two minutes or 100 MB gets slow to
  download and re-encode; vidscroll warns in the console. Trim it or prepare it
  with the CLI.
- **Background loops that play on their own.** A plain `<video autoplay muted
  loop>` does that with less work.
- **YouTube, Vimeo and similar watch pages.** A page that plays a video isn't
  the video file, and their streams can't be read by other sites. Any direct
  link to a video file works, on your own site or on a CDN or another domain.
  Files on other domains scrub best when the server allows CORS.
